Cellular respiration is the process by which cells break down glucose to release energy stored in its chemical bonds. It occurs in three main stages: glycolysis, which takes place in the cytoplasm and splits glucose into two molecules of pyruvate while producing a small amount of ATP; the citric acid cycle, which occurs in the mitochondrial matrix and further breaks down pyruvate while generating electron carriers like NADH and FADH2; and oxidative phosphorylation, which takes place across the inner mitochondrial membrane and uses those electron carriers to produce the majority of the cell's ATP through the electron transport chain and chemiosmosis. Unlike photosynthesis, which captures energy from sunlight, cellular respiration releases stored chemical energy, and the two processes are often described as complementary because the products of one supply the reactants of the other.
The invention of the printing press by Johannes Gutenberg in the mid-15th century transformed the spread of information across Europe. Before movable type, books were copied by hand, a slow and expensive process that limited literacy mostly to clergy, nobility, and scholars. Gutenberg's press made it possible to produce many identical copies of a text quickly, which sharply lowered the cost of books and allowed ideas to circulate far more widely than before. This had ripple effects well beyond religion and scholarship: it helped standardize languages, accelerated the spread of scientific discoveries, and later played a role in the Reformation by allowing pamphlets and translated Bibles to reach ordinary people directly. Historians often point to the printing press as one of the clearest examples of a single technology reshaping not just how information moved, but who had access to it.
